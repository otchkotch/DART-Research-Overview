Reply to Paul Renzi’s research review

Paul, thank you for the detailed critique. Your strongest points are the need for an explicit mission, comparable competing systems, qualified detection range, and complete deployment economics. Those belong at the center of this investigation.

This is a public research response to issues #38 and #39. It records agreement, corrections, and proposed next work. It does not approve a new mission, hardware design, deployment topology, or performance claim. DART has not demonstrated its desired approximately 60-second warning capability.

Where we agree

  • Define useful warning. A candidate mission must specify the aircraft and approach conditions, protected boundary, acceptable missed detections and false alerts, required bearing information, and what a recipient can do with the warning. Fixed-site warning is a credible candidate; it is not yet the selected mission.
  • Range is a major economic driver. Required deployed quantities depend on qualified detection and directional coverage under the relevant noise, weather, terrain, and failure conditions. A cheap node with inadequate coverage can produce an expensive system.
  • Compare complete systems. Gateways, power, mounting, communications, installation, maintenance, spares, operator attention, and useful life belong alongside node cost. Vendor claims, creator demonstrations, manufacturing estimates, and independently measured performance need separate labels.
  • Use existing evidence before building. Published measurements and component specifications should narrow the decisions first. A small experiment is valuable when its result can change a specific engineering choice.

Aperture: the numerical correction matters

The historical reference is a regular tetrahedron with a 33 mm body edge and microphones at its face centers. Its pairwise acoustic-center separation is 11 mm. Microphones at the vertices of a 33 mm tetrahedron would be a different configuration.

Theoretical quantity11 mm spacing33 mm spacing
Maximum pair arrival-time difference, d/c32.1 μs96.2 μs
Maximum pair phase difference at 200 Hz2.31°6.93°
Maximum pair phase difference at 1 kHz11.55°34.64°

These calculations use c = 343 m/s and a far-field plane wave. The roughly 0.1 ms figure applies to 33 mm acoustic spacing, not 11 mm. A small low-frequency phase difference is a real difficulty, but does not by itself establish that useful bearing estimation is impossible. Usable harmonics, signal-to-noise ratio, channel phase response, synchronization, geometry, and the estimator all matter.

Larger aperture improves ideal timing sensitivity while introducing frequency-dependent ambiguities and packaging tradeoffs. No geometry wins from aperture alone. The next comparison should retain directional capability rather than treating a single presence microphone as an equivalent replacement.

Competitors and cost: compare like with like

The approximately $197.54 DART figure is a prototype purchasing model: $98.54 of priced or carried-forward hardware, $39 of unquoted material allowances, and $60 of assumed assembly labor. It is not a factory quotation or installed-system cost. The $30 goal remains aspirational.

  • VolAnti is a useful low-cost reference, but its published architecture sums four microphones and explicitly does not estimate direction. Its creator reports approximately 104 m in a particular field demonstration and £50–80 in parts. That does not establish equivalent directional coverage or a general probability-of-detection range.
  • Neuron Soundware’s Sound Shield describes a €100–150 production-cost estimate and a sub-1 W sensor, alongside a hub and central platform. That estimate should not be compared directly with DART’s mixed retail-component and labor basket without accounting for shared infrastructure and production assumptions.
  • The Zvook NW0 seller listing provides useful range, spacing, and power claims. They remain supplier claims until the test conditions and acceptance endpoints support a matched comparison.

At $197.54 each, 135 nodes cost about $26,668 and 189 cost about $37,335, before shared infrastructure or deployment. These are scenario costs, not approved quantities. For one evenly spaced line, node hardware cost per kilometre is 1,000 × node price / spacing in metres. Rings, overlapping directional coverage, failure tolerance, and multiple lines require their own geometry.

A unique “DART must exceed 70–100 m” competitiveness threshold is not established without matched coverage requirements and lifetime costs. Likewise, we should avoid declaring a universal wind penalty or ruling out a deployment class from broad battlefield assertions alone.

The proposed Saturday test: useful screening, limited conclusion

The published trip/wire weekend plan uses a speaker at a fixed 16 ft distance and changes playback level to emulate greater distances. That can screen the recording, detector, logging, and radio workflow. It cannot establish outdoor drone detection range: level reduction does not reproduce the changing propagation path, atmospheric absorption, wind, ground interference, source directivity, or real aircraft variation.

Its acknowledged follow-on real-flight layer is therefore essential for a range claim. Three detections out of three trials also provide little statistical assurance: under independent identical trials, the one-sided exact 95% lower bound on detection probability is only about 37%. Four quiet minutes with zero false alerts give an approximate one-sided 95% upper bound of 45 false alerts per hour under a stationary Poisson model. Those are theoretical confidence calculations, not measured trip/wire performance.

A shared recording and metadata format could be useful. Participation, scheduling, hardware purchases, and any specific joint trial have not been committed here.

The smallest work that could change the decision

  1. Write candidate mission envelopes for review. Keep range, latency, bearing accuracy, false-alert exposure, environment, and responder action explicit. Do not silently write a preferred mission into the established baseline.
  2. Build a matched evidence and cost table. Include presence versus bearing capability, measured versus claimed range, conditions, availability, node and shared-system costs, and uncertainty. The NATO 2025 RFIP does contain 300 m threshold and 500 m objective detection language for a stated target class. It is a discovery request, not a DART acceptance requirement or procurement commitment.
  3. Resolve the aperture question analytically first. Use measured drone spectra and realistic channel-noise and mismatch specifications to compare face-centered, vertex, and other four-microphone geometries. An ideal calculation is a sensitivity bound, not field qualification.
  4. Then measure only unresolved decision variables. If published evidence cannot settle channel phase stability or outdoor bearing accuracy, a synchronized four-channel comparison with known source direction can address that gap. If qualified range remains decisive, a controlled real-source transect with recorded conditions, background exposure, held-out recordings, and stated detection criteria can address it. Choose sample size from the confidence needed for the decision.

The immediate opportunity is to reduce uncertainty in the mission and complete-system economics while investigating simpler electronics, power, packaging, and assembly. Removing directional information would change the capability being priced.
